Design mock-up — the About text of a work, revision 2 — approved by the maintainer on 2026-11-19, with the recommendations below. The details pane gains two things: the work’s synopsis in its head, and an About section with the long text, its language and its source. The same section is drawn in the library and in Remotes. The rules behind it (the fields of metadata schema 3, the Markdown subset, the source and the push guard, recipes, the command line) are in the design note backlog/docs/design/about-text.md, accepted the same day with every recommendation of its own questions.

Changes in revision 2, after the maintainer’s answers

  • The about text is Markdown in a small subset, and the pane draws it: headings, lists, emphasis, code, links. What is outside the subset is shown as typed; the specimen row shows both.
  • A link opens only after a question that names the host and the whole address. Nothing is fetched by the program.
  • More… and Less count drawn blocks, not characters.
  • The second language’s sample is an English placeholder under the tag nl: the repository is written in English.

What is drawn — approved behaviour

  1. The synopsis in the head. Under the title and the tag line the pane shows the work’s synopsis in the viewer’s language: one plain paragraph, at most 500 characters, no markup read in it. The release’s own description follows in the muted style, as This release: …, when it says something the synopsis does not. A record of metadata schema 2 has one synopsis without a language; it is shown as English (Commander Keen 4 here).
  2. The About section. A section of the details pane, between the head and the pane’s other sections, for a machine image, an installation set, a machine whose image has titles, and a tag selected in Remotes: one widget everywhere. An image with several titles has one per title. A title without an about text has no section and no placeholder.
  3. The text is Markdown, drawn. Paragraphs; headings, every level drawn as one, in the style of the pane’s section headings; bullet and numbered lists of two levels, with • and – as bullets; block quotes beside a bar; a thin rule; emphasis, strong emphasis and code; links, written as [text](https://…), <https://…> or a bare https:// address. Lines are at most about 72 characters long, whatever the pane’s width.
  4. Outside the subset: shown as typed. Raw HTML, an image, a link to anything but an https address, and the rest of Markdown are drawn as the characters the author wrote, never hidden and never an error. The specimen row shows them. The author sees a warning for each when the recipe is checked.
  5. Collapsed and open, counted in drawn blocks. Collapsed, the section shows the text up to and with its second body block: a paragraph, a list or a quote. A heading or a rule is drawn where it stands and is never the last thing shown. A list of more than six items shows its first five and a line with “…”. More… follows when anything is left out and opens the rest in place; the pane scrolls. Less closes it again. A text that fits has neither (Ken’s Labyrinth here).
  6. A link asks first. A click on a link, or Enter on a focused one, opens Open link?: the host on a line of its own, the whole address in a field, and Open in browser, Copy address and Cancel, which is the default button and what Escape does. It is asked every time, for every host. Hovering a link shows its address in the status bar. The program itself fetches nothing: no image, no preview, no check that the address answers.
  7. The source line. Small muted print under the text, always drawn when the text is, also while it is collapsed. Own text: Own text, and the authors when the record credits any. A licensed source: From “title” by authors · the licence · changed: what; the title is a link to the source and the licence a link to its page at creativecommons.org, and both ask as every link does. No source: a warning line that says where the text may go. A kind of source this release does not know is shown as written.
  8. Languages. The viewer’s catalogue languages are one ordered preference (Tools › Preferences… › General), set by the first control above. The section shows the first language of the preference the title has, then English, then the first language it has. When that is not the first preference it says so above the text: Not available in Italian; shown in English. A title with several languages has a switch in the section’s heading; a choice there lasts while the title is selected and changes no preference. The source line belongs to the language shown. The synopsis is looked up the same way, on its own, and says nothing when it falls back. Nothing is machine-translated. Languages are named in English.
  9. Remotes. A selected tag shows the same card and the same section. The listing knows only the manifest: its description annotation, which is the release’s description. The synopsis and the about text come from the tag’s title record, which the pane already reads for the edition and the version. Until the record arrives the card shows the description and Reading the title record…; if it cannot be read the card keeps the description and says so. The pane’s last line names what was read. No disk, cover or other layer is fetched before Pull. A text from a registry is drawn by the same rules, and its links ask the same question.
  10. Hover text and search. A row’s hover text in the library lists is the synopsis in the viewer’s language; in Remotes the manifest’s description, and the synopsis once this session has read the tag’s record. Find: matches the words of the synopsis and of the about text in every language, without the markup and without the addresses of links.
  11. Unchanged. Everything else in the panes, the lists, the tree, the menus and the toolbar. Edit metadata… does not edit the about text in this step.

Asked, and decided by the maintainer with the recommendation (2026-11-19)

  1. Where does the synopsis sit? Drawn: in the head, with the release’s description under it as This release: …. Alternative: the synopsis as the first paragraph of the About section. Recommendation: as drawn. A title without an about text still says what it is.
  2. How much Markdown? Drawn: what point 3 lists. Left out and shown as typed: code blocks, forced line breaks, HTML, images, reference links, tables. Recommendation: as drawn. Headings of any level drawn as one means an author’s # or ## just works; code blocks can be added later without changing a stored text.
  3. Strong emphasis without a bold face. The desktop loads no bold face and draws its headings in the strong text colour. Drawn here in bold, as the mock-ups draw headings. Recommendation: the strong colour for strong emphasis and for the text’s headings now, emphasis in the slanted face egui makes; a bold and an italic face are font work of their own.
  4. How is “shown as typed” drawn? Drawn: in the body style, like the text around it, with no mark. Alternative: a muted tone for what was not understood. Recommendation: as drawn. A reader should not have to learn a second kind of text; the warning belongs to the author’s check.
  5. How much is shown collapsed? Drawn: up to the second body block, a long list cut to five items; no count of characters. Alternative: whole blocks while they fit ten lines at the pane’s width, which collapses the same text differently on two screens. Recommendation: as drawn.
  6. More… in place, or in a reading window? Drawn: in place, and the pane scrolls. Recommendation: in place, as agreed; a reading window can follow if a long text in the pane proves awkward.
  7. The link question. Drawn: asked every time, Cancel the default, no “do not ask again”, no list of trusted hosts. Recommendation: as drawn. Opening a link is rare, and the question is the one place the real host is shown.
  8. The words of the source line. Drawn: Own text · authors and From “title” by authors · CC BY-SA 4.0 · changed: abridged, always visible. CC BY and CC BY-SA ask for the credit beside the text, so it cannot hide behind More…. Recommendation: as drawn.
  9. The language switch. Drawn: a drop-down in the section’s heading, only for a title with several languages; its choice lasts while the title is selected. Alternative: no switch, the preference alone decides. Recommendation: the switch.
  10. Names of languages. Drawn: in English (Dutch, Italian), and the tag for a language the program has no name for. Native names would bring characters the bundled fonts do not have. Recommendation: English names.
  11. A language the fonts cannot draw (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, right-to-left scripts). Not drawn. Proposed: the switch lists it as cannot be shown by this release, and the fallback skips it. Recommendation: as proposed; fonts and text shaping are separate work.
  12. Remotes before the record arrives. Drawn: the manifest’s description, then the synopsis takes its place above it. Alternative: a copy of the synopsis on the manifest, so the card never changes; it would change the manifest of every published set that has a synopsis, and holds one language. Recommendation: as drawn.
  13. A text without a source. Drawn: shown, with a warning line under it. It stays in this library and on private registries; the push guard refuses it everywhere else. Recommendation: as drawn, so the owner sees why a push will be refused before trying one.

Out of scope

  • Writing and editing the text in the desktop: a later step with its own mock-up. Today a recipe’s work file holds it, inline or in a file beside it.
  • The preference page for the viewer’s languages, drawn with the other pages of Preferences.
  • The Push… dialog’s refusal for a text without a source: it reads as the dialog’s other refusals do.

Drawn, not written: the bar beside a block quote and the rule are painted lines; a link is underlined text in the link colour, with no mark after it; emphasis is the slanted face. Open link? has no picture.

Letters. Every sample is English and ASCII, so this page adds no letter to the inventory. Real catalogue text brings its own characters; they are data, not mock-up glyphs, and question 11 covers the ones the fonts lack.
